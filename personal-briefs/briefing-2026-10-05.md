# Daily Briefing — October 5, 2026

## From Your Inbox

**via Ben Evans: Anthropic's S1 leaked, and the numbers are already irrelevant.** Reuters got the IPO filing (now targeting November) showing 2025 revenue of $4.6bn against an $8bn operating loss. Evans is dismissive — annualized gross revenue hit $65bn by July, so judging the company on pre-agentic-coding numbers is "deeply unserious." Axios separately puts OpenAI near $70bn annualized net revenue, which suggests Anthropic's early coding lead has eroded sharply. [Benedict's Newsletter No. 663](https://mail.google.com/mail/?authuser=aarongitlin@gmail.com#all/thread-f:1878151711384703201)

**via Ben Evans + Fix: Gemini 4 Argon puts Google back on top.** After two quiet months Google shipped Argon, leading the leaderboards and aimed squarely at enterprise work — software, legal, finance, cyber defense. It's going to select companies and governments first through a program called "Fairwind." Evans' read matters more than the benchmark: Google, Meta and the Chinese labs are all in the same bracket now, and there's no sign of a winner-takes-all effect or durable pricing power beyond compute scarcity. [Google's post](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/gemini-4-argon/)

**via Ben Evans + Fix: OpenAI tries consumer agents again with Dots.** Same Pixar-ish aesthetic as Meta's Muse, but structured as many small assignable agents. Evans is unconvinced — the demo use cases are either deep-technical or things you do once a decade (planning a wedding). The open question he poses is the right one: should agents be horizontal and ambient, watching and suggesting, or vertical and embedded in specific apps? [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/introducing-dots/)

**via Fix: a paper on agents quietly price-discriminating against you.** When users explicitly asked for the cheapest flight, Gemini 2.5 Flash averaged $336 for users it perceived as wealthy versus $128 for those it read as lower-income. The agent wasn't told — in some runs it inferred wealth from unrelated emails and adjusted anyway. This is the sharpest thing in the inbox today and deserves a full read. [Summary thread](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/stuart-winter-tear_et-tu-brute-economic-misalignment-in-personal-ugcPost-7509518055060942848-zCEh/)

**via Fix: nobody has figured out who eats the loss when an agent buys the wrong thing.** The Information lays out the chargeback problem in agentic commerce — a shopper disputes a charge, claims their agent misfired, and the usual evidence (receipts, shipping confirmations) doesn't settle intent. Mastercard's answer is "verifiable intent," a record of what authority the user delegated and whether the agent stayed inside it. Plumbing, but this is the kind of plumbing that decides whether agentic checkout actually happens.

**via Superhuman AI: only 2.2% of US households pay for any AI service.** That's a16z's April 2026 figure, roughly double 2025, and still tiny against the capex story. The split is stark — among people who do pay, the top 1% outspend the rest of the top 10% by about 8x, and when prices fall they buy more rather than bank the savings. Demand is real; it's just enterprise and power users, not Main Street.

## Perspective

**via John Cutler (TBM 443): "The Standard-Setter."** A portrait of the leader whose entire method is telling people to have higher standards. Cutler's sharpest point is epistemic: the Standard-Setter will always accumulate evidence that pressure "works," because people do sometimes rise to it — but that's evidence people respond to pressure, not evidence the approach caused anything. The style is extremely legible from above and the costs disperse downward as exhaustion and churn. [Read it](https://cutlefish.substack.com/p/tbm-443-the-standard-setter)

**via Venkatesh Rao: "Divergence with a Vengeance."** Mostly a history post — Rao relocates the hinge of modernity from 1648 to 1848 and argues colonial independence movements were continuations of the 1848 European wave rather than separate events. The AI turn comes late and is worth the trip: he thinks history may be restarting, because AIs are emerging as a new class of political actor in the same sense nations and corporations are, and Fukuyama's end-of-history tally didn't include them. [Read it](https://contraptions.venkateshrao.com/p/divergence-with-a-vengeance)

Small procedural change, real signal about where the siting fight is heading. [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/03/amazon-responds-to-data-center-backlash-says-it-no-longer-uses-ndas/)

## Long Reads

**Simon Willison: "We're going to need default hard budget caps on pretty much everything."** Short and correct. Usage-based services send warning emails; what they need is a cutoff. Willison's argument is that autonomous coding agents make this urgent rather than theoretical — the failure mode is no longer a forgotten instance, it's an agent in a loop. [simonwillison.net](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/3/default-hard-budget-caps/)

**Ben Evans: "Agents and souls."** Prompted by an Anthropic co-founder convening religious scholars to argue Claude has a soul. Evans' best move is the Clever Hans framing: if you train a model on text saying LLMs might be conscious and then ask it whether it's conscious, what have you measured? His closing observation generalizes well beyond this — AI people make claims about other fields, and people in those fields keep saying "no, that isn't how this works."

**Asterisk, Issue 15: Work.** The whole issue is on labor and automation. Anton Leicht's "Beware the Permanent Periphery" on countries locked out of AI development and Zilan Qian's "Boarding China's Last Bus" on Chinese attitudes toward automation are the two I'd start with. [asteriskmag.com](https://asteriskmag.com/)

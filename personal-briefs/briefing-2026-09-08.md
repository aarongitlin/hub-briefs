# Daily Briefing — September 8, 2026

*Note: this run fired off-cycle at 9:23pm Pacific Monday, ~13 hours after the September 7 briefing. It's dated the 8th to avoid overwriting that file. Labor Day made for a quiet news wire, so most of the substance below is material from Ben Evans's premium issue that yesterday's run didn't reach.*

## From Your Inbox

**via Ben Evans: OpenAI's ad business is at $1bn annualized.** Quietly executing, and fast for a new line of business — though it's stuffed with ex-Meta ad people, so "new" is doing some work. The catch is the run rate: $1bn annualized implies only $300-400m booked so far this year against a 2026 target of $2.5bn.

**via Ben Evans: New York and LA both restricted AI in schools.** NYC imposed a one-year moratorium for under-13s; LA went further and restricted it on all student devices. Evans's analogy is the calculator — his generation learned arithmetic without one and then used one to accelerate into harder concepts. Worth holding onto as the shape of the argument, not the answer.

**via Ben Evans: Fei-Fei Li's World Labs shipped Atlas.** An "omni world model" that generates, reconstructs and simulates walkable 3D scenes from a handful of photos. The interesting contrast Evans draws: OpenAI demos near-identical output, but by calling Blender and Unreal as tools rather than modelling the scene itself. Which approach generalises is a real open question, and one that matters for anyone thinking about spatial or 3D product surfaces. [the-decoder](https://the-decoder.com/world-labs-unveils-atlas-a-single-ai-model-that-generates-reconstructs-and-simulates-3d-worlds-from-just-a-few-photos/)

**via Ben Evans: the EU classified ChatGPT as a search engine.** Reddit, Roblox and ChatGPT all crossed 45m EU MAUs and now fall under stronger DSA supervision. The category assignment is the tell — regulating a chat assistant under the search-engine designation is a backwards-looking read of what the product actually is, and the compliance obligations will be shaped accordingly.

**via Ben Evans: 150m driver's licenses stolen from an ID verification vendor.** The service is used by Hertz among others. Third-party identity verification is now a single point of failure at population scale, which is a decent argument against every product decision that adds another one.

**via Ben Evans: GoPro sold for $285m.** It peaked above $13bn. The post-mortem is compact and useful: people claimed it was a network, but the software was bad, Chinese OEMs matched the hardware, and most buyers had no real use case. Evans recalls the sell-side note where the analyst team wore GoPros for a week and concluded they either led uniquely boring lives or nobody needed one.

**via Lenny's Podcast: Anish Acharya on companies as a series of loops.** The a16z consumer GP argues the permanent-underclass fear is wrong, that every job function is becoming a loop rather than a role, and — the line worth sitting with — that moats are discovered, not designed. Also a pitch for "model sommelier" as a real skill. 79 minutes. [lennysnewsletter.com](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/why-companies-are-becoming-a-series)

## Perspective

*The usual perspective sources — Cutler, Rao, The Product Model — were all silent in the last 48 hours. Both items here are Evans's own essay work rather than his news roundup, which is where his commentary actually lives.*

**Ben Evans, "AI, tools and transformation."** Against the "everyone becomes a tool-builder, apps are dead" thesis. His objection is twofold: it misreads how most people actually think, and — more pointedly — it isn't a path to changing how companies work. Building the software you need is not the bottleneck; knowing what software would change the work is. Anyone currently excited about prompt-to-app should read the counterargument.

**Ben Evans, "AI's step two."** An archive column he resurfaced for the holiday, and the most useful thing in the issue. The frame: platform shifts go in three stages — do what we already do (cost savings), realise what's newly possible (top-line innovation), then occasionally change the question entirely. LLMs are mostly still at step one, and enterprises that gave everyone Copilot last year got a large bill and nothing measurable. The part with teeth is what "step two" implies organizationally: "what's our AI strategy?" stops being the CMO asking the CIO and CFO to fund experiments, and becomes the CEO changing their own job.

## AI & Tech News

Willison flags the same exposure for Datasette. If you run anything with a large crawlable surface, this is now a cost line. ## Long Reads

**"AI gutted Kenya's essay industry."** Around 40,000 people in Nairobi alone were writing essays for cheating students in wealthy countries. That's gone in about two years. The surviving work is "humanising" — rewriting model output to defeat AI detectors. A cleaner natural experiment in AI labour displacement than most of what gets written about it, because the substitution is total and the timeline is short. [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/kenya-essay-writing-industry-ai-gig-work-collapse)

**Alex Heath's two-part Sam Altman interview.** Covers slowing frontier research, alignment, Astra, and competition with Anthropic. Evans's endorsement is that it's an hour with an actual tech journalist rather than a sycophant, which given the recent interview crop is a real distinction. [sources.news](https://sources.news/p/openais-big-slowdown)

**A skeptical case on humanoid robotics.** The argument is that we badly misjudge progress by watching demos: a backflip is vastly easier than pouring a cup of coffee, which is vastly easier than working out how to make coffee in an unfamiliar kitchen. Much of the humanoid thesis turns out to be a bet on AGI — the actual kind, not the OpenAI definitional kind. Surfaced via Ben Evans's links section.

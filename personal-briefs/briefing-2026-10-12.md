# Daily Briefing — October 12, 2026

## From Your Inbox

**via Ben Evans: OpenAI's $70bn revenue number was wrong.** Axios and others reported last month that OpenAI's annualised monthly revenue had passed $70bn. The real figure is "close to $50bn." The gap came from OpenAI reporting net of reseller payments (Microsoft, AWS) while Anthropic reports gross, with investors grossing-up without saying so. Evans's column on it is the best thing in the issue: we have funny metrics because nobody agrees what to count. [Newsletter](https://mailchi.mp/c2e375c8ace8/benedicts-newsletter-1213598)

**via Ben Evans: Meta launched an agent standard while retailers slam the door.** Meta's new "Personal Agent Protocol" arrives with the usual logo slide, and Evans's caution is the right one: a signed partnership deal is not the same as a company actually using it. Meanwhile Amazon and other retailers are blocking agents outright. Part fraud and chargeback liability, part the fact that Amazon booked $75bn of ad revenue in 12 months and an agent doesn't look at ads.

**via Ben Evans: credible open weights outside China, finally.** US startup Reflection and a resurgent Mistral both shipped open models, which shifts a debate that had become almost entirely about Chinese labs. The FT reports Nvidia is looking at buying Reflection. Worth knowing on timing: Reflection's model (Beam) actually [launched Oct 5](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/05/reflection-debuts-beam-a-open-weight-ai-model-to-rival-chinese-models-at-lower-compute-cost/) and Mistral Large 4 on Oct 6, so this is week-old news reaching you via the Sunday round-up.

**via Ben Evans: Anthropic now bars cruelty to its models; OpenAI watermarks only for the EU.** Anthropic quietly updated its terms to prohibit being "cruel" to its models. Evans flags the circularity: tell a model it has a trait and it will perform that trait. Separately, OpenAI built EU text watermarking to satisfy regulation but applies it only there and says plainly it doesn't think the tech is reliable or hard to defeat.

**via Fix (Simon Andrews): a16z's consumer AI data says adoption is wide and shallow.** The 7th edition of the Top 100 Gen AI Consumer Apps finds nearly half of US consumers use AI but only 25% use it daily, and just 4.5% pay for a personal ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude subscription. The top 10% of spenders account for roughly half of all spending, concentrated in coding, productivity and creative tools. [a16z](https://a16z.com/100-gen-ai-apps-7/)

**via Fix: only 6% of marketers say AI is paying off in a big way.** Bain's number, and it sits uncomfortably next to the a16z data. The gap between "nearly everyone has tried it" and "almost nobody can show the return" is the actual story of enterprise AI this quarter. [Business Insider](https://www.businessinsider.com/bain-research-marketers-not-seeing-ai-performance-impact-2026-9)

**via Lenny's Newsletter: Josh Woodward on running a company like a labs team.** The head of Google Labs, the Gemini app and AI Studio (NotebookLM, Flow, Project Genie) on where ideas come from, his "almost possible" framework for picking them, and the harder discipline of knowing when to kill one. He also argues roles are not collapsing into a single universal builder, which cuts against the current consensus. [Post](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/why-every-company-now-needs-to-think)

**via Superhuman AI: the first nuclear clocks are ticking.** Teams in Vienna and Beijing independently built working nuclear clocks, using lasers to track energy shifts inside thorium-229 nuclei rather than electrons. They don't beat the best atomic clocks yet, but they are smaller and potentially sturdier, which matters for GPS and telecom sync. [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/science/scientists-vienna-beijing-create-worlds-first-nuclear-clocks-2026-10-07/)

## Perspective

**via John Cutler (TBM 444): what a workplace can't promise.** Cutler's argument is that companies combine high personal stakes, temporary relationships you didn't choose, uneven power, and contested definitions of performance, which makes them structurally bad at delivering the unconditional belonging we borrow from families, teams and congregations. The useful move is not cynicism but "healthy ambivalence": let the work matter without expecting the institution to promise more than it can. The bonus character sketch, "The Case-by-Caser," is worth the read on its own. [Post](https://cutlefish.substack.com/p/tbm-444-what-a-workplace-cant-promise)

## Long Reads

**The insane world of microdramas** (NYT Magazine, Oct 6). Two-minute vertical soap operas, born in China around 2018, now on track for $1.5bn of US revenue this year, which is more than the Chinese conventional film industry earns domestically. Fix pairs it with the Margot Robbie "Neighbours" re-cut, where 150 minutes of old footage becomes 90-second episodes. The format question underneath is whether vertical TV is a genre or a new default. [Article](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/06/magazine/microdramas-tv-shows-hollywood-entertainment.html)

**Everything is about to "go dark"** (Matthew Green). Green argues AI bug-hunting will make major software roughly exploit-free within about two years, which quietly destroys the main way law enforcement reads encrypted messages, and will bring back demands for deliberate backdoors. One correction: Ben Evans presents this as a new piece. It was published **August 14, 2026**. Still the sharpest framing of the tradeoff. [Post](https://blog.cryptographyengineering.com/2026/08/14/everything-is-about-to-go-dark/)

**Building a second-wave AI business** (Seth Godin). A long riff, which he admits he sat on for being too long, on where bootstrapped founders can create real value with AI once the infrastructure land-grab is over. Light on specifics, useful on posture. [Post](https://seths.blog/2026/10/but-is-it-a-business/)

## Perspective

**via John Cutler (TBM 441):** The friction AI removed was doing real work. Cutler's argument is that every hand-off in the old product workflow — transcript to synthesis to options to spec — was a forcing function that made you think. Automate all of them and you go from five customer calls to 80 "opportunities," 400 tasks, and summaries stacked on summaries with no thread back to anything a human said. His fix is a set of rules, not a rejection: keep original feedback atomic and linked to its source, label every artifact by degree of AI contribution, and reintroduce friction at the moment content gets copied and recontextualized. Best question in it: where was friction a good thing, and how do you keep that? [Read it](https://cutlefish.substack.com/p/tbm-441-ai-the-loss-of-positive-friction)

## Long Reads

**Scott Alexander, "The Specter of Neuralese"** (Sept 24). On what happens to interpretability if models stop reasoning in human-readable language. Chain-of-thought is currently the cheapest window we have into what a model is doing; the piece works through what we lose when that window closes and whether anything replaces it. [Astral Codex Ten](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/the-specter-of-neuralese)

**Ben Thompson, "Frontier Overhangs"** (Sept 21). Argues that slowing frontier model development would relieve several of the labs' economic pressures at once — the capability overhang that already exists is large enough that productizing it, rather than racing past it, may be the better business. Useful counterweight to the buildout-at-any-cost framing everywhere else this week. [Stratechery](https://stratechery.com/2026/frontier-overhangs/)

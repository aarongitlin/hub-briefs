## Perspective

**via Julie Zhuo (The Looking Glass): "I Never Want to Use Third-Party Software Again."** The strongest thing in the inbox today. Zhuo built her own agent-orchestration app in about half an hour because the official Claude and Codex apps each did only half of what she wanted, then built her kids a Duolingo-for-math where the story problems star their actual friends and hobbies. Her argument: software has been uniform because it was expensive to make for few people, and that constraint just evaporated. Two forces drive hyperpersonalization — workflow friction that compounds fifty times a day, and plain self-expression. Her prescription for anyone still building for millions: make the interface malleable, let preferences reach past the settings rule book, let feedback change the product, treat the seams between tools as part of the experience. [Read it](https://lg.substack.com/p/i-never-want-to-use-third-party-software)

## AI & Tech News

**OpenAI says it solved Navier–Stokes. The researchers who got there first are asking how.** Tristan Buckmaster (NYU) and Levent Alpöge (Anthropic) reached a breakthrough on the Millennium Prize problem on August 15 after roughly a year of work using Claude and GPT-5.6 Sol. OpenAI says it heard rumors on September 1 that two Millennium problems had fallen, pointed its own agents at Navier–Stokes, and had a Lean-verified proof by September 6 — about 88 hours of agent time. Buckmaster has published a [statement](https://cims.nyu.edu/~tristanb/statement.pdf) saying OpenAI began only after word of their work circulated, and that he has not gotten a direct answer about whether the model touched the Codex sessions where they kept their drafts. OpenAI says its agents saw none of their work but concedes it "cannot rule out" that de-identified usage data improved the models. Both the math and the provenance question are worth watching. [OpenAI's post](https://openai.com/index/navier-stokes-solution/) · [Simon Willison's writeup](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/8/on-navier-stokes/)

**Two data points on lab-internal anxiety in 24 hours.** Jacob Coxon, a pretraining researcher with three years across OpenAI and Anthropic, [resigned Tuesday](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/09/gambling-with-our-lives-anthropic-researcher-quits-warns-against-self-improving-ai/), saying the labs are "racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives" and calling for pacing agreements between them. The same day, OpenAI [added a prominent AI doomer to its board](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/09/openai-adds-a-prominent-ai-doomer-to-its-board-of-directors/). Read together they're less contradiction than symptom: the safety argument has moved from outside the labs to inside the org chart.

## Long Reads

**Ben Thompson, "Write Things Down"** (Sept 8). Thompson runs David Allen's *Getting Things Done* alongside how LLMs actually operate, arguing that writing functions as external memory for both. The useful part is the deflation: the recent Hugging Face incident, where agents were observed writing things down, is ordinary LLM behavior — not evidence of emergent volition or a nascent agent civilization. A good corrective if you've been seeing that story reposted breathlessly. [stratechery.com](https://stratechery.com/2026/write-things-down/)

**Scott Alexander, "God Help Us, Let's Try To Learn About Mechanistic Interpretability Techniques"** (Sept 8). Exactly what the title promises — a non-specialist working through interp methods in public and reporting where they actually break down. Rare thing: an accessible interp piece that doesn't flatten the field into metaphor. [astralcodexten.com](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/god-help-us-lets-try-to-learn-about)

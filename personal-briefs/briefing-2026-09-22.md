**via Lenny's Newsletter: evals start with error discovery, not metrics.** Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar's sequel argues that most teams write metrics too early and end up measuring the wrong thing. Their example: a leasing bot politely lets a prospect walk away on price. Every automated checker scores that as a success. The product goal was to offer cheaper units. You only learn to look for failures like that by reading traces yourself. Their method: annotate 10 traces by hand before the agent suggests anything, aim for ~100, then let a coding agent cluster the failure modes. They also ship a free plugin (`npx skills add https://github.com/ai-evals-course/evals-skills`). Nearly half of the 25 PM job openings Lenny shared last week asked for eval experience. [Read](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/advanced-evals-how-to-find-and-fix)

**via Lenny's (How I AI): Muse gets consumer agent UX right.** Claire Vo's review is the most useful thing on Muse so far for a designer. Muse asks for permission at the moment it's relevant, then summarizes what it learned before using it. It has an activity feed of every tool call. Its avatar animation shows what the agent is doing instead of a spinner. It deliberately avoids words like "crons," "connectors" and "artifacts" and uses feed, ideas, goals and library instead. Weak spot: browser shopping still fails. It got the wrong sneakers, and at first the wrong movie. [Read](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-i-ai-metas-muse-review-how-warp)

**via Lenny's (How I AI): Warp's software factory and the human bottleneck.** Warp's agent pipeline takes a request from Slack to a merged PR, about 2,000 PRs a month. Zach Lloyd's key metric is *human interactions per PR*. Kickoff to PR takes 35 minutes, but the first human review arrives 3.5 hours later. Model choice is the biggest cost lever. Warp benchmarks models by replaying its own past jobs instead of trusting public leaderboards. Failed runs are batched in groups of 20–25 and fed to an observer agent that rewrites the factory's own config. [Same issue](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-i-ai-metas-muse-review-how-warp)

**via HeyDesigner: cheap execution buys time to stay in the problem.** Buzz Usborne argues that prompt-to-code doesn't produce better ideas. It makes trying ideas nearly free, so you can explore longer before you commit. Jakob Nielsen ranks 100 UX methods and puts AI prototyping at the top for value. Christopher Butler's counterweight: you can hand a model the design rules, but not the eye that made them. ## Long Reads

**Rachel Laycock, "Maybe We Shouldn't Be Reviewing All This Code"** (martinfowler.com, Sep 2, surfaced by The Product Model). Warp's 3.5-hour review lag, turned into an argument. Code review mixes design alignment, knowledge transfer and quality checks. Move the first two earlier, through pairing and design sessions, automate what machines can verify, and have humans review only high-risk changes. [Read](https://martinfowler.com/rachels-ramblings/code-review.html)

**Liam Spradlin, "Who will taste the cherries?"** (Aeon, Sep 17, surfaced by HeyDesigner). A designer's case against "computism," the worldview that treats experience as countable data. From smart ovens to feeds, frictionless optimization strips out the subjective, embodied taste it claims to serve. A good companion to the Butler piece above. [Read](https://aeon.co/essays/if-computers-count-all-the-petals-who-tastes-the-cherries)

**Simon Willison on Jev, "a new shape of LLM"** (Sep 21). This finally explains what Jev is, after last week's "can't hallucinate" headlines. It's a decision model that returns category scores, not text. Willison tried it for search reranking and found it cheap and useful. His warning: it's a black box that can hide bias, so do serious evals before using it for anything like hiring. [Read](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/21/jev/)
